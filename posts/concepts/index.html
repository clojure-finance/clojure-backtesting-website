You do not need a finance background to use the backtester, but a few ideas come up on every page of this documentation. This page explains them once, with examples from the bundled sample dataset.

What a backtest is

A backtest runs a trading strategy on historical data as if it were happening live. On each day the strategy may only use information that was available on that day. It places orders, the backtester fills them at realistic prices, and at the end you see how much the strategy would have made or lost, and how risky the ride was.

A backtest is a cheap way to find out that an idea does not work. It cannot prove that one will: markets change, and a strategy tuned to fit the past often fails on new data. Treat good results as a reason to test further, on other periods and other securities.

The shape of every backtest

  1. Load the data. load-dataset points the library at a directory of daily prices (the main dataset) and, optionally, company fundamentals (the Compustat dataset).
  2. Start a portfolio. init-portfolio sets the start date and your starting cash. Calling it again starts over.
  3. Repeat for every trading day. Your code looks at today's data, decides, places orders with order, and calls next-date to move to the next trading day. The library has no built-in loop: you write it, usually with while or dotimes.
  4. Close positions. end-order sells everything at today's close and ends the run.
  5. Review results. Print the order record, portfolio and evaluation report, or plot them.

Here is the smallest useful version:

(load-dataset "resources/sample-data/main" "main" add-aprc)
(init-portfolio "1990-01-02" 10000)

(order "10001" 50)          ; buy 50 shares
(dotimes [_ 10]             ; let 10 trading days pass
  (next-date)
  (update-eval-report))
(end-order)                 ; sell everything

(print-portfolio-record -1)

The clock: "today" and the date pointer

The backtester has a single global clock, the date pointer. get-date tells you where it is. Every data function answers relative to it: get-info returns today's rows, get-prev-n-days returns earlier ones, and indicators such as moving-avg are computed from today backwards.

The clock only moves forward, one trading day per call to next-date, skipping weekends and holidays (any date not in the dataset). No function returns data from after "today", so your strategy cannot accidentally use tomorrow's prices. This mistake is called look-ahead bias, and it is the most common way backtests produce results that are too good to be true.

When an order fills

An order does not fill when you place it. You place orders after seeing today's closing price, and you can no longer trade at a price that is already in the past. So the order becomes pending, and fills at the close of the next trading day:

This is why the order record of the first backtest shows each order a day after it was placed. A few more rules:

  • If the security has no price on the next day, the order waits, and expires after 3 trading days by default.
  • An order is a number of shares. Positive buys, negative sells. With :remaining true the number is the holding you want to end up with, so (order "10001" 0 :remaining true) closes the position.
  • On the same day, sales fill before purchases, so the cash from a sale can pay for a purchase.

The Data and Trading Rules page lists every rule.

Securities and the sample dataset

Each security is identified by its PERMNO, a permanent number assigned by CRSP, the data provider the library is designed for. Permnos are strings in the API, such as "10001". The documentation uses permno and security for the same thing; permno-tic and tic-permno translate to and from ticker symbols.

The sample dataset in resources/sample-data has three made-up securities over January to March 1990, each with a quirk that exercises part of the library:

PermnoTickerPrice rangeQuirk
10001AAAabout $44 to $52Pays a dividend on 1990-02-15
10002BBBabout $115 to $135, then $62 to $68 after the splitSplits 2-for-1 on 1990-03-01
10003CCCabout $18 to $21No prices from 1990-02-05 to 1990-02-09

Prices: raw and adjusted

Every row has two prices that matter:

  • :PRC, the raw closing price. Orders trade at this price, and the indicators are computed from it.
  • :APRC, the adjusted price, added by add-aprc. It follows the security's total return, so dividends and splits do not make it jump.

The difference shows on BBB's split. After 1990-03-01 one old share became two new ones, so the raw price halved while the adjusted price carried on. Two fills from the RSI example, before and after the split:

|      :date | :permno | :price |  :aprc | :quantity |
|------------+---------+--------+--------+-----------|
| 1990-02-22 |   10002 | 133.11 | 133.11 |      -5.0 |
| 1990-03-02 |   10002 |  66.92 | 133.84 |       5.0 |

The portfolio adjusts the share count on a split (5 shares become 10), and with reinvested dividends it also buys extra shares on dividend days, so neither event changes what your holding is worth. Indicators use the raw price, so a split looks like a crash to them. The Golden Cross example shows the effect.

Long, short and borrowed money

A long position is shares you own: you profit if the price rises. A short position is shares you borrowed and sold: you profit if the price falls, because you can buy them back cheaper. In the portfolio a short position has a negative quantity.

You can also buy more than your cash pays for. The shortfall is a loan, shown as negative cash. Borrowed cash and borrowed shares both magnify gains and losses, so the backtester limits them:

TermMeaning
Equity, total valueCash plus long positions minus short positions: what you would have after closing everything
Gross position valueLong positions plus short positions, both counted as positive
Leverage(loan + short positions) / equity
Marginequity / gross position value. 100% means no borrowing; lower means more borrowed
Initial marginThe margin a new borrowing order must keep, 50% by default. Orders that would go below it fail
Maintenance marginThe floor for the margin while you have borrowed, 25% by default. Below it, every position is closed (a margin call) and the run ends

Example. With $300 of cash, buying 10 AAA shares at $50.81 costs $508.10. The portfolio borrows $208.10, so leverage is 208.10 / 300 = 0.69 and margin is 300 / 508.10 = 59%. That is above the initial margin of 50%, so the order goes through. The Buying on Margin example follows this portfolio further.

Measuring performance

update-eval-report adds today's metrics to the evaluation report. The metrics are:

MetricWhat it tells you
Daily returnHow much the total value changed from yesterday, as a log return: ln(today / yesterday). Log returns add up over days
Total returnThe sum of the daily log returns since the start
VolatilityHow much the daily returns vary (their standard deviation). Higher means a bumpier ride
Sharpe ratioAverage daily return divided by volatility, scaled to a year (× √252). Return per unit of risk; above 1 is usually considered good
Maximum drawdownThe largest fall from a peak in total value, as a percentage of that peak. The worst loss you would have sat through
Profit per trade(total value − starting cash) / number of filled orders, not counting the orders end-order places

The rolling versions (r-vol, r-sharpe) use only the last 30 trading days, to show how risk changes over time. The Evaluation page has the exact definitions.

TermMeaning
